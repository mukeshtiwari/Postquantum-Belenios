{\large\bfseries PQ-Belenios: AI-Assisted Formal Verification for the Post-Quantum Migration of Verifiable Electronic Voting}\\[2pt]
{\small Mukesh Tiwari, Fran\c{c}ois Dupressoir, Kerstin Eder, Nirav Ajmeri (University of Bristol), Kristian Gj{\o}steen (NTNU),\\
V{\'e}ronique Cortier, Pierrick Gaudry (CNRS, Inria, LORIA)}
\end{center}

\section{Vision}
In a Belenios election~\cite{cortier2019belenios}, every encrypted ballot and the corresponding zero-knowledge proof 
of its validity are published on a public website
so that \emph{anyone} can verify the result. That openness is a strength today and a liability tomorrow: the
encryption and zero-knowledge proofs rest on the discrete logarithm assumption, which a quantum computer will
break. Anyone can download the ballots now and, once such a computer exists, learn how each person voted
(\emph{harvest now, decrypt later}). Ballot secrecy must last a lifetime, so the move to post-quantum
cryptography cannot wait for the NCSC's 2035 deadline~\cite{ncsc2025pqc}.

Post-quantum replacements for these primitives exist, but they are much harder to get right.
The lattice-based mix-net of Aranha et al.~\cite{aranha2023mixnet} (CCS 2023) came with a security proof, yet Bootle 
et al.~\cite{bootle2025mixnets} showed that a malicious mixer can change votes without
being detected. The proof used a step that holds over fields but fails over the rings used in lattice cryptography.
The same bug appears in an earlier paper~\cite{aranha2021shuffle} and a later one~\cite{hough2025cic}, and passed
three rounds of peer review. Bootle et al.\ propose a fix, but their proof is again on paper only and may hide a
similar gap. Machine-checked proofs catch such errors, but today each one takes experts years; at that rate, almost
nothing will be verified before 2035. \textbf{Our vision is that AI agents, supervised by experts and checked by the Rocq and 
the EasyCrypt proof assistant, turn machine-checked post-quantum migration from a multi-year research project into a task of months.} 
Our post-quantum cryptography experts will start from the lattice-based proof of shuffle of Aranha et al.~\cite{aranha2021shuffle},
co-authored by Gj{\o}steen, a member of this team, and the mix-net built on it~\cite{aranha2023mixnet}.
Their definition and security proof go through our AI-assisted machine-checking pipeline, where AI agents attempt the
formal proof in proof assistants Rocq and EasyCrypt; the proof assistants are the final arbiter. A proof assistant requires
every step to hold for all values, so a step that fails for some values leaves the agents with a goal they cannot close,
and that goal points to a counterexample. Our formal verification experts first confirm that the goal is truly unprovable;
our post-quantum experts then fix the flaw by revising the definition and the proof, and the revised versions go through
the pipeline again.
The outcome of this iterative process is a post-quantum mix-net with a machine-checked security proof. Belenios is our 
proving ground because it is a widely deployed 
and heavily scrutinised system~\cite{cortier2017sp,cortier2018belenioseasycrypt}; its code base was also reused to build 
the official independent verifier for the 2022 French legislative election for overseas voters, commissioned by the 
French foreign ministry~\cite{vvfe,cortier2023french}.

\section{Approach}

\subsection{The domain challenge, and why it needs AI}
The challenge comes from the domain experts. Cortier and Gaudry designed Belenios and maintain it~\cite{cortier2019belenios}.
Gj{\o}steen co-designed the lattice shuffle and mix-net at the centre of this
project~\cite{aranha2021shuffle,aranha2023mixnet}. Cortier, Dupressoir, and Tiwari built the machine-checked proofs
and verifiers of classical Belenios~\cite{cortier2017sp,cortier2018belenioseasycrypt,haines2019verified,287095}.
Their question is: \emph{given a voting system whose classical version already has machine-checked proofs, how do we
get a machine-checked security proof for its post-quantum version, at a small fraction of the original expert
cost?} Experts cannot close this gap alone. Moving a proof from classical groups to lattice rings means
re-establishing every lemma in a new algebraic setting and adding noise bounds, norm reasoning, and approximate
soundness, where the extractor recovers only a short witness rather than an exact one. This work is large, repetitive,
and error-prone, and the flaw described in Section~1 is exactly the kind of error it produces. AI can do this work safely
because the proof assistant is the final arbiter: its kernel rejects every incorrect proof.
Recent results show that this is now realistic. Agentic AI has produced EasyCrypt proofs in hours that previously
took experts months~\cite{ma2026shannonprover}, and has written Rocq proofs of crash-freedom for production software
at a scale of 180 million lines~\cite{anthropic2026formal}. Both report the same limitation: a proof is only as good
as its specification. Neither has tackled the probabilistic, lattice-based security proofs that the post-quantum
migration needs; this project takes that step. The project sits in the call's \emph{quantum technologies}
priority area, on its security side: quantum computing is the reason every deployed public-key system must be replaced.

\subsection{Scope: from classical to post-quantum Belenios}
Belenios supports ElGamal encryption, Schnorr signatures, Chaum--Pedersen and disjunctive zero-knowledge proofs,
Pedersen threshold key generation, and the Terelius--Wikstr\"om shuffle~\cite{terelius2010restricted}. It counts
simple approval and list votes through homomorphic tallying, and complex methods (Condorcet--Schulze, Single
Transferable Vote, and Majority Judgment) through verifiable mix-nets. We start with the primitives that matter most
for ballot secrecy and integrity: ballot encryption, the proof of shuffle, verifiable decryption, signatures, and
secure channels.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Standardised primitives with existing machine-checked proofs.} We move ballot signatures to ML-DSA
and trustee channels to ML-KEM. Both are NIST standards whose security proofs are already machine-checked in
EasyCrypt~\cite{barbosa2023dilithium,almeida2024mlkem}. ML-DSA derives its keys deterministically from a 32-byte
seed, so Belenios can still derive a voter's signing key from their private credential. This is mainly integration work.
\item \textbf{Lattice protocols for the mix-net (the core of the project).} BGV encryption~\cite{bgv2012}, BDLOP
commitments~\cite{baum2018bdlop}, a lattice shuffle proof, and verifiable distributed decryption give a post-quantum
version of every step of a Belenios mix-net election. None of these lattice protocols has a machine-checked proof.
Their classical analogues do (ElGamal and the Terelius--Wikstr\"om
shuffle~\cite{cortier2018belenioseasycrypt,haines2019verified,287095}), and those took years to develop. This makes
Belenios a fair test of whether AI can transfer an existing machine-checked proof to the post-quantum setting.
\end{itemize}
By the end of the project, a Schulze, STV, or Majority Judgment election in Belenios can run on post-quantum primitives
from start to finish with its core cryptographic components machine-checked.

\subsection{Methodology and work packages}
Tiwari leads the project and delivers most of the technical work, in Rocq and in the agent pipeline, working
closely with Dupressoir on EasyCrypt. The AI connects to both proof
assistants through model-agnostic Model Context Protocol (MCP) tool servers, so we can compare frontier and
open-weight models and avoid depending on one vendor. We use two proof assistants because the project needs two
kinds of proof. EasyCrypt's probabilistic relational logic suits the game-based privacy and soundness proofs, and
the classical Belenios proofs already use it~\cite{cortier2017sp,cortier2018belenioseasycrypt}. Rocq extracts
verified OCaml code that plugs into Belenios and has the algebra libraries the ring reasoning needs; our classical
verified verifiers are written in it~\cite{haines2019verified,287095}. Running the agents on both also tests whether
the method generalises beyond a single tool. All work packages follow the loop set out in Section~1: the post-quantum
experts supply definitions and proofs, the agents attempt to machine-check them, the formal verification experts
(Tiwari, Dupressoir, Cortier) confirm that any goal left open is genuinely unprovable, and the post-quantum experts
revise; we iterate until every proof closes. Timings and deliverables are on the workplan page.

\textbf{WP1: The mix-net as formal statements (Gj{\o}steen, Cortier, Tiwari; M1--M5).} Starting from the definitions
and proofs of the lattice shuffle~\cite{aranha2021shuffle} and of the mix-net and verifiable decryption built on
it~\cite{aranha2023mixnet}, AI agents draft EasyCrypt and Rocq models. Their security statements reuse the
ballot-privacy and verifiability definitions of the classical Belenios
proofs~\cite{cortier2017sp,cortier2018belenioseasycrypt}, so that classical and post-quantum guarantees are directly
comparable. The domain experts review every model in two specification sprints, one at NTNU and one at LORIA. Output: \emph{PQVote-Bench}, an open
benchmark of lemmas, each paired with its classical counterpart where one exists, with a held-out test split and
recorded expert baselines (D1.1).

\textbf{WP2: Agentic proof and expert revision (Dupressoir; M3--M11).} We extend the ShannonProver
approach~\cite{ma2026shannonprover} from standard primitives to lattice protocols: agents split each theorem into
game hops and lemmas and search for EasyCrypt proof scripts, guided by prover feedback. Agents retrieve the matching
classical lemma (e.g.\ special soundness of Chaum--Pedersen) and adapt it to its lattice counterpart, repairing it
from kernel error messages~\cite{first2023baldur}. When a lemma resists proof, the agents use the open goal to search
for a counterexample and machine-check it. The formal verification experts confirm that the goal is genuinely
unprovable rather than merely beyond the agents; the post-quantum experts then revise the definition and the proof,
and the revised version goes through the pipeline again. The first iteration has a known answer: the pipeline must refute the flawed shuffle
argument of~\cite{aranha2021shuffle} by exhibiting ring elements with $a_1a_2=b_1b_2$ that are not a permutation of
each other. For the repair, the experts can draw on the general permutation argument of Bootle et
al.~\cite{bootle2025mixnets}, adapted to the re-encryption setting. We run controlled
comparisons (no AI; single-shot model; agentic; agentic with transfer) and record the share of lemmas closed
automatically, expert hours, and compute cost. Outputs: machine-checked security proofs of the revised shuffle and mix-net (D2.1), evaluation report (D2.2).

\textbf{WP3: Verified post-quantum verifier for Belenios (Tiwari, Gaudry; M4--M12).} With the WP2 agents, we formalise the
verification equations of the revised shuffle and decryption proofs from WP2, prove them correct in Rocq
against the WP1 specification, and extract an OCaml verifier, following our classical verified
verifiers~\cite{haines2019verified,287095}. Gaudry leads the integration of ML-DSA, ML-KEM, and the verified verifier
into the Belenios code base, building on the team's experience turning Belenios into the official verifier for the 2022
French legislative election~\cite{vvfe,cortier2023french}. We test the verifier on transcripts from a post-quantum
Belenios prototype. The two proof assistants meet at the verification predicate: EasyCrypt proves that acceptance
implies a correct shuffle, and Rocq proves that the extracted verifier computes that predicate. We write the
predicate once in Rocq, translate it to EasyCrypt with AI assistance, and validate the translation by expert review
and by evaluating both versions on shared test vectors. A fully formal EasyCrypt-to-Rocq bridge is beyond this
project and is part of the roadmap (D4.2). Output: verifier, integration into the experimental post-quantum version of Belenios, and test harness (D3.1).

\textbf{WP4: Trustworthy AI-assisted verification (Ajmeri, Eder; M1--M12).} The proof assistant
rejects any incorrect AI-produced proof, so the remaining risk lies in the specification: an AI-drafted
theorem may be vacuous, or may describe the wrong protocol. Large-scale AI verification efforts report
exactly this failure mode, including agents that weaken a specification so that it becomes easier to
prove~\cite{anthropic2026formal}. WP4 therefore requires a named domain expert to sign off every
AI-drafted or AI-modified specification, and runs automated checks on each one: \emph{specification
mutation} (a deliberately broken protocol must fail to verify) and vacuity tests. Specialist agents (formaliser,
prover, critic, auditor) work under explicit norms on who may change a specification and when a human must sign
off. WP4 also logs the provenance of every AI-produced artefact. Outputs: workflow guide and failure taxonomy (D4.1),
roadmap (D4.2).

\subsection{Building on prior work}
The project reuses substantial existing assets rather than building foundations: the EasyCrypt privacy and
verifiability proofs of Belenios~\cite{cortier2017sp,cortier2018belenioseasycrypt} (Cortier, Dupressoir); the
open-source Belenios code base~\cite{cortier2019belenios} (Cortier, Gaudry); the Rocq verified
verifiers and machine-checked Terelius--Wikstr\"om and Bayer--Groth shuffle proofs~\cite{haines2019verified,287095}
(Tiwari); the lattice voting protocols~\cite{aranha2021shuffle,aranha2023mixnet} (Gj{\o}steen); the machine-checked
ML-KEM and ML-DSA proofs~\cite{almeida2024mlkem,barbosa2023dilithium}; and the ShannonProver agentic
system~\cite{ma2026shannonprover} (Dupressoir). The grant pays for the new part: AI-assisted transfer to the
post-quantum setting.

\subsection{Effectiveness: success criteria}
We will count the project a success if (i) the pipeline refutes the known-flawed shuffle argument and the
revised shuffle and mix-net have machine-checked security proofs; (ii) at least 60\% of PQVote-Bench test lemmas close
without human-written proof steps; (iii) expert time falls at least five-fold against the recorded baselines; and
(iv) an end-of-project review by Cortier and Gaudry, who did not design the lattice protocol, finds no accepted specification defect.
Negative results are also valuable: a precise map of where current AI fails on lattice proofs (e.g.\ norm reasoning
or probabilistic coupling) is a deliverable in its own right.

\subsection{Feasibility and risk management}
Tiwari, as project lead, chairs a fortnightly technical meeting and a monthly steering meeting with all co-leads.
\emph{(1) AI fails on lattice-specific reasoning.} A month-6 go/no-go (M2) narrows the scope to component lemmas,
and the failures are documented as a research result. \emph{(2) AI-drafted specifications are subtly wrong.} Expert
sign-off is mandatory, and WP4 mutation and vacuity checks run before any lemma enters PQVote-Bench. \emph{(3) The
revised proof cannot be completed.} This is a finding, not a failure: either the agents locate a further gap, which
the experts use in the next revision, or the unprovable step is documented. \emph{(4) Model access or cost changes.} MCP interfaces are
model-agnostic, with an open-weight fallback. \emph{(5) Dependence on one main technical contributor.} Each work package
has a named co-lead, and Dupressoir can take over the agent pipeline if needed.

\subsection{Translating outputs into impact}
PQVote-Bench, the agent pipeline, MCP tool servers, proofs, and verifier will be released under open licences.
Cortier and Gaudry, who maintain Belenios, have agreed to include the project's verified outputs (the post-quantum
primitives and the verified verifier) in an experimental version of Belenios that uses post-quantum algorithms, giving them a direct route to real
elections; we will also offer contributions to EasyCrypt. Any flaw found in a published
or deployed design will go through coordinated disclosure with its authors before publication. A closing workshop in
Bristol (M12) will bring together cryptographers, formal-methods and AI researchers, and election stakeholders. The
roadmap (D4.2) sets out how the workflow transfers to other systems that must migrate by 2035, such as messaging,
identity, and payments, and will form the basis of a follow-on proposal.

\subsection{Research environment}
Bristol brings together the disciplines the project needs: formal verification of voting (Tiwari), the
cryptography and EasyCrypt group (Dupressoir), the Trustworthy Systems Laboratory (Eder), which works on the
verification of AI-enabled systems, and research on accountable multi-agent systems (Ajmeri). NTNU (Gj{\o}steen) is a leading centre for lattice-based voting protocols, and
LORIA in Nancy (Cortier, Gaudry) is where Belenios is developed and maintained. Tiwari will make research visits to
NTNU and LORIA for the specification sprints.
